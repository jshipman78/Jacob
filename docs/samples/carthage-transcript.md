# CARTHAGE MUST BE DESTROYED

_Rome really did destroy Carthage as completely as any ancient city was ever destroyed — but almost every vivid detail people repeat about that destruction, from the salted fields to the death tolls to the idea that Rome had plotted it for years, was added, inflated, or simplified after the fact, and the real event is smaller and stranger than the legend built on top of it._

## SOWN WITH SALT

**[0:00]** Roman soldiers are said to have leveled Carthage in 146 BC, then plowed salt into its fields, acre by acre, so nothing would grow there again for generations.

**[0:13]** No ancient writer who was actually there ever recorded it happening.

**[0:19]** This is Carthage — and if its most famous ending turns out to be invented, what actually happened? Carthage must be destroyed.

## A CITY ROME COULD NOT FORGET

**[0:29]** Carthage sat on the coast of modern Tunisia, and by the mid-second century BC it had rebuilt itself into a thriving, sophisticated city, decades after Rome first crushed it. Nobody agrees how many people actually lived there.

**[0:45]** Inside Rome's Senate, opinion split. Cato the Elder was said — by the biographer Plutarch, writing two centuries after Cato died, since no contemporary source confirms it — to end every speech with those words, though the exact Latin, Carthago delenda est, appears nowhere in ancient texts and is a later distillation of his account. Scipio Nasica argued the opposite, that Carthage should be spared, but that too comes from Plutarch, writing generations later — tradition, not transcript, and Nasica's own motives are only thinly documented.

## THE THIRD WAR

**[1:25]** In 149 BC, Rome declared war. Two consuls led the opening years of the siege — Manius Manilius and Lucius Marcius Censorinus — then Lucius Calpurnius Piso took command after them. None of them could break the city. The siege dragged on for two years under the consuls before command passed, in 147 BC, to a young officer named Scipio Aemilianus, who led the final phase to the city's fall in 146.

**[1:58]** In 146 BC, his troops finally broke through the walls. What followed was days of fighting, street by street, room by room. Then the city burned — ancient sources differ on how long, with the fire said to have lasted anywhere from about ten days to as long as seventeen. Watching it, according to the eyewitness historian Polybius, Scipio wept and quoted a line from Homer's Iliad.

## WHAT THE RECORD ACTUALLY SAYS

**[2:26]** Polybius was there, and what he wrote is narrower than the myth: the dead, he said, were ‘incredibly large’ in number, not that Carthage was ‘utterly exterminated’ — a phrase that actually describes Rome’s earlier destruction of Macedonia. The ash agrees with him. Excavators have found burned, collapsed buildings under thick ash dated to 146 BC, right where he placed the fire.

**[2:53]** The population he saw vanish is itself disputed: the geographer Strabo claimed seven hundred thousand people, while the historian B. H. Warmington called that impossible, arguing the true figure was closer to two hundred thousand, though possibly nearer four hundred thousand a century earlier. Appian put seventy thousand dead in one battle alone — a number even the source preserving it calls probably exaggerated. One modern estimate puts total deaths at a minimum of a hundred fifty thousand, built on a population scholars place anywhere from under a hundred thousand to over seven hundred thousand. No ancient source gives a total death count, other modern tallies run several times higher, and the fifty thousand survivors who came out of the city are recorded with no breakdown by age or sex.

## PREMEDITATED OR PROVOKED

**[3:49]** The traditional reading casts Rome as a predator that had been circling for years, tilting arbitration rulings against Carthage in its border dispute with Numidia to manufacture the pretext for a war Rome intended to fight regardless. It is a tidy story. That story doesn't survive contact with the record.

**[4:11]** A 2018 reassessment of those rulings found no consistent pattern of Roman bias at all, and argues some decisions on record actually favored Carthage over Numidia — though other historians still hold that Rome's arbitration systematically favored Numidia, so this remains contested. The war looks messier than the myth. A similar picture holds for how total the destruction really was — Roman farmers were already resettling Carthaginian land within decades, even as the city itself waited nearly a century to be rebuilt, which is a long way from the salted, sterilized wasteland usually traced to nineteenth-century writers — though a few scholars point to a much older, ambiguous medieval reference, so the legend's true origin is still debated.

## THE CITY THAT DID NOT NEED SALTING

**[5:03]** A siege that dragged on for years, a breached wall, and days of house-to-house killing and fire were already brutal enough to end a city, whatever the messy politics behind it or the disputed numbers that followed. Carthage did not need salt to die.

**[5:21]** The precise tolls, the salted fields, the years of Roman premeditation — those got stitched onto the record afterward, by people retelling the story for their own reasons. That destruction was punishment enough on its own. It never needed the myth.

## THE STORY UNDER THE STORY

**[5:39]** Almost every great historical story has a real event buried somewhere underneath layers of exaggeration, myth, and people rewriting the record to make themselves look better.

**[5:52]** Carthage didn't just fall. It became a case study in how the record gets simplified, sharpened, and sold as certainty long after the fire went out.
