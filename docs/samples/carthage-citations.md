## Narration audit

This script is unusually careful — nearly every disputed figure (Cato's quote, Nasica's rebuttal, the fire's duration, Strabo vs. Warmington, Appian's 70,000, the 150,000 estimate, the arbitration bias question, and the salt legend's origins) carries the qualification the fact-check called for, often in language lifted almost verbatim from the required hedges. The dates, command sequence, and casualty/survivor figures all trace cleanly to the verified claims, and nothing on the CUT list resurfaces. The weak points are small: the cold-open 'Carthage must be destroyed' is dropped in as an unattributed, unhedged quote before the film has earned the right to say it, a couple of sentences in the archaeology and casualty passages add unverified specificity (a precise spatial match to Polybius's fire, an unsupported 'no breakdown by age'), and the closing treatment of the salt legend's origins compresses two distinct sourced strands (a few scholars citing an ambiguous 1299 document; newer research pointing to North African tradition) into one vague phrase, quietly dropping the 'newer research' thread. None of these rise to a debunked myth being resurrected as fact — they're erosion at the edges of an otherwise well-sourced script.

- **MEDIUM · LOST HEDGE** — “Carthage must be destroyed.”
  - This disputed quote (C7) is dropped in as a bare, unattributed assertion with no framing at all — no 'said to,' no mention that it comes from Plutarch two centuries later, no note that the exact Latin never appears in ancient texts. That qualification only arrives several minutes later in 'A City Rome Could Not Forget.' A viewer stopping at the cold open takes it as a verified quotation.
  - Suggested: Either cut the line here and let the later, properly hedged sentence introduce it, or add a light qualifier on first use, e.g. a title card reading 'Carthage must be destroyed' — or so legend says Cato always said.
- **MEDIUM · OVERREACH** — “Excavators have found burned, collapsed buildings under thick ash dated to 146 BC, right where he placed the fire.”
  - C23 supports ash deposits and violently destroyed structures dated to 146 BC, but says nothing about the excavation sites corresponding to a specific location Polybius described. 'Right where he placed the fire' claims a precision of spatial corroboration between the archaeology and the ancient text that isn't in the verified claim.
  - Suggested: Excavators have found burned, collapsed buildings under thick ash dated to 146 BC — physical confirmation of the destruction Polybius described.
- **LOW · UNTRACED** — “No ancient source gives a total death count, other modern tallies run several times higher, and the fifty thousand survivors who came out of the city are recorded with no breakdown by age or sex.”
  - C22 establishes that Appian gives no breakdown by sex for the 50,000 survivors; it says nothing about age. Adding 'or age' asserts something the claim list doesn't cover.
  - Suggested: ...and the fifty thousand survivors who came out of the city are recorded with no breakdown by sex.
- **MEDIUM · LOST HEDGE** — “A similar picture holds for how total the destruction really was — Roman farmers were already resettling Carthaginian land within decades, even as the city itself waited nearly a century to be rebuilt, which is a long way from the salted, sterilized wasteland usually traced to nineteenth-century writers — though a few scholars point to a much older, ambiguous medieval reference, so the legend's true origin is still debated.”
  - C3's hedge requires two distinct threads: (1) a few scholars citing an ambiguous 1299 papal document, and (2) separately, newer research arguing the legend may have older roots in medieval North African tradition. The script collapses both into one vague clause attributed only to 'a few scholars,' silently dropping the 'newer research'/North African tradition strand entirely.
  - Suggested: ...though a few scholars point to an ambiguous 1299 papal document, and newer research argues the legend may have older roots in medieval North African tradition — so its true origin is still debated.
